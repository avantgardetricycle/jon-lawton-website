The long version

Jon Lawton has been at this a long time.

Decades of writing, recording, and playing blues, country, and folk, on National Steel, banjo, 6- and 12-string guitar, or whatever else a song calls for. Get the whole story →

Jon's music career started with classical piano lessons at eight, the kind of rigid instruction he'll tell you he mostly resented at the time, in an Orange County that in those years was still mostly citrus groves and dirt roads. Guitar came later, in the eighth grade, and the influences that came with it were folkier than what he'd eventually become known for: Crosby, Stills & Nash, Joni Mitchell, James Taylor, the singer-songwriter records that were everywhere in the early '70s.

What changed things was a cousin who had tickets. Jon saw the Rolling Stones three times in Los Angeles as a teenager, and the Grateful Dead not long after, standing in front of their wall of sound. Somewhere in there the blues shuffle got its hooks in him, and it never let go.

These days he moves between National Steel, 6- and 12-string guitars, banjo, and a little slide Dobro. He chooses the instrument a song actually needs rather than sticking to one out of habit. Onstage that shows up as a kind of restlessness, in a good way: rags into stomps into his own quieter, more observational tunes, carried by a voice that talks to a room as much as it sings to it.

Ask him who he's been listening to for fifty years and the list doesn't really change: Big Bill Broonzy, Brownie McGhee, John Lee Hooker, Hank Snow, Steve Earle, Pete Seeger, Merle Haggard. It's an odd enough combination that by now it mostly just sounds like him.

In a 2014 interview about the blues as a foundation for everything else, he put it simply:

“The blues is an elemental building block of music.”Jon Lawton, in a 2014 interview
